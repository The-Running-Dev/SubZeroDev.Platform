## F1
Severity: BLOCKING
Status: accepted risk
Claim: The vendor configuration package cannot handle the nonstandard sign-out behavior that #94 requires it to cover.
Where: Data model § 10; Control flow path 4; Alternatives considered §§ 11–12; contract Unresolved item 4.
Breaks when: A product uses a provider such as the Auth0 case in #94, whose logout requires a vendor-specific URL. The proposed vendor package can supply bearer-validation settings only; Platform has no sign-in or sign-out path, and path 4 assigns the logout URL to each client. The proposed proof compares configuration-source output with settings-file output, so it never exercises logout.
Consequence: Every client must implement the same vendor-specific sign-out behavior itself, reproducing the hand-wired setup that #94 was opened to eliminate. The issue's sign-out proof and named provider-package behavior remain impossible under this design even though Unresolved item 4 is treated as settled.
Cost: Cheap to resolve before the vendor-package contract is published; expensive later because clients will have acquired their own provider-specific sign-out integrations.

## F2
Severity: BLOCKING
Status: brief conflict
Claim: A public claims-bearing principal leaves token-sourced grants structurally possible for consumer providers.
Where: Data model § 3; Failure modes, consumer permission provider; Alternatives considered § 10; `Principal.Claims` and `IPermissionProvider.GrantsAsync`.
Breaks when: A consumer provider reads an `admin` role from `Principal.Claims` and returns its permissions while the same bearer token remains valid for an hour. The evaluator cannot see the grant's source, and the consumer can omit the `Platform.Testing` harness. Revoking the role during that hour leaves the next request allowed.
Consequence: The written prohibition can be breached without a type or runtime boundary stopping it. This contradicts #92's requirement that the normalized principal carry no role or permission data and that token-bound authorization be structurally impossible; the original delayed-revocation failure remains available to every consumer.
Cost: Cheap to decide while the principal and provider contracts are still under review; expensive later because removing or constraining a public claims surface would affect consumers built against it.

## F3
Severity: STRUCTURAL
Status: defect
Claim: A revocable mirror can pass the proposed test while real issuer-role revocation remains delayed.
Where: Data model § 3, issuer-managed role mirror and consumer-owned freshness; Control flow path 4, next-request revocation claim; contract Unresolved item 3.
Breaks when: A consumer mirrors issuer roles into its grant store every 15 minutes. An operator removes a user's issuer role at 12:00, the user presents the still-valid token at 12:01, and the provider reads the old role from the mirror. A harness that revokes the local mirror row and then checks denial still passes.
Consequence: The operator cannot remove that user's access on the next request even though the provider follows the proposed source rule and passes its revoke-then-deny test. The design's universal revocation promise depends on a freshness obligation it leaves to each consumer without a stated bound.
Cost: Cheap to settle before multiple consumers adopt the mirror pattern; expensive later because each consumer may have a different synchronization delay and operational promise.
